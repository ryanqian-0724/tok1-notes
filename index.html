Lesson 1

Introduction to TOK

Source: TOK1 Lesson 1 slides

TOK in IB terms
“Knowing about knowing.” A course about critical thinking and inquiring into the process of knowing, rather than learning a specific body of knowledge.
  • A core element every DP student takes (at least 100 hours of class time).
  • TOK and the DP subjects should support each other.
In Chemistry you learn what an atom looks like; in TOK you ask how scientists can know about something nobody has ever seen, and why the atomic model has changed over time.
How TOK works
  • There is no fixed compulsory content — TOK consists mostly of questions, and there are rarely “right” answers.
  • Disagreement is likely; it is not unpleasantness or a competition in cleverness.
  • You don’t need to be a philosopher to be good at TOK. Being more confident to talk ≠ being more intelligent.
  • You will practise making your case, standing your ground and justifying your opinion in front of others.

Course structure

CourseContent
TOK1The compulsory core theme: Knowledge and the knower
TOK2Optional themes (Religion, Technology, Language, Indigenous knowledge, Politics) + TOK Exhibition
TOK3Areas of knowledge: History, Human sciences, Natural sciences
TOK4Areas of knowledge: Mathematics, the Arts + TOK Essay

Marks

  • Max IB score is 45; 3 points come from the EE and TOK combined.
  • TOK grade = Essay 67% (written in DP2, TOK4) + Exhibition 33% (DP1, TOK2).
  • You cannot get the diploma without passing the core components.

The core theme: Knowledge and the knower

You will explore what it means to know and what knowledge is, examine the factors that shape how you make sense of the world, discover where your values come from and what shapes your perspectives, and reflect on how the communities you belong to shape how you construct, share and evaluate knowledge.

  • What do the words BEAUTY, SUCCESS and NORMAL mean to you — and where did your understanding of them come from?
  • To what extent can you know yourself?
  • In what contexts is it possible for others to know you better than you know yourself?
Lesson 2

Who Is the Knower?

Source: TOK1 Lesson 2 “Who is the knower” slides + your notes

You as a knower
Knowledge cannot exist without some sentient being doing the knowing. Every time you learn, absorb or produce new knowledge, you are somehow affected by it. You learn about climate change in Biology (absorb), write an essay on it (produce), and then start cycling to school instead of being driven (it affects you).
Personal identity
The idea you have about yourself, which develops throughout your life (e.g. nationality, religion, political views, interests).
  • It is contingent and transient (see below).
At 10 you might have seen yourself as “a football fan”; at 17 as “a future engineer who cares about the environment”.
Contingent only in your notes
Only true under certain conditions or contexts → “true if X”. “I am a Finnish student” is only true if you live and study in Finland — move abroad and it changes.
Transient only in your notes
Temporary, prone to updates and replacements → true for now. Until 2006, students learned there were 9 planets; then Pluto was reclassified and it became 8.
Personal knowledge
Sometimes acts as a bridge between our personal experiences and our personal identity. As you get more experiences, your knowledge grows and your identity develops. Volunteering at a hospital (experience) teaches you how patients are cared for (knowledge), and you start to see yourself as someone who wants to study medicine (identity).

Slide task: give an example from your own life for each arrow, in each direction (six examples).

  • What knowledge matters to you, and where do your values come from?
  • What things do you know that nobody else could know?
  • What knowledge do you consider important / not seek / not accept / are willing to “edit”?

Knowledge communities & tribalism

Knowledge community
Groups of people with common interests and shared knowledge. Your IB Chemistry class, a chess club, the community of climate scientists, an online forum of Minecraft builders.
Tribes
The groups you identify with most strongly. Your loyalty to and dependence on your tribe tends to influence you strongly, e.g. by shaping your perspectives. You’re in a chess club (a knowledge community), but your national identity or religion may be a tribe — part of who you are.
Tribalism
The inherent bias towards the group we identify with. Fans call a foul against their own player “obviously unfair”, but the same foul by their team “part of the game”.
  • Is loyalty to your tribe a valid justification for taking a friend’s side in a dispute when you know they are wrong? Or is such bias unethical?
  • Is there a correlation between the groups that most shape your identity and the groups that most affect your emotions?

Conformity and the lack of it

Conformity only in your notes
The tendency of individuals to change their behaviours, beliefs or attitudes to match the accepted norms of a group. It affects the individual knower and is linked to communities of knowers. One can also not conform. Solomon Asch’s line study (from the slides): participants gave an obviously wrong answer about which line was longest because everyone else in the room had. When even one other person disagreed with the group, the real participants stopped conforming.
Knowledge initially rejected
New ideas often face resistance before becoming widely shared knowledge. (Book: RLE Muhammad Ali 1.2, p. 6; exercise 1.3.) Ignaz Semmelweis said doctors should wash their hands between patients (1847). He was mocked and lost his job; only after germ theory (Pasteur, Lister) was the idea accepted.
  • To what extent do our thoughts and ideas reflect those of the people around us? How do we decide when we need to “swim against the tide”?
  • What factors stop new knowledge from being accepted and becoming widely shared?
Lesson 4

Subjectivity & Objectivity

Source: TOK1 Lesson 4 Subjectivity and Objectivity slides + your notes

The 12 key concepts

They are the analytical tools for the whole course: in TOK you don’t study new subject facts, you examine how we know what we know.

Why they matter

  • Analytical vocabulary — precise language to dismantle and critique knowledge claims.
  • Conceptual bridges — connect and compare areas of knowledge (e.g. the Arts and Natural sciences).
  • Assessment — examiners explicitly look for them in the Essay and Exhibition.

How to use them

  • Formulating KQs — e.g. “Is this news story fake?” → “How does perspective influence the interpretation of historical events?”
  • Analysing the framework — e.g. how power affects which scientific research gets funded.
  • Evaluating claims — test justification, evidence and the limits of certainty.
Certainty
How sure we are about a knowledge claim. Very certain that 2 + 2 = 4; fairly certain it will rain if the forecast says 90%; uncertain about what happened before the Big Bang.
Culture
The shared beliefs and values of a group that shape how we think. In some cultures, looking a teacher in the eye shows respect; in others it is rude.
Evidence
The data or facts used to back up a claim. “Smoking causes cancer” is backed up by decades of studies comparing smokers with non-smokers.
Explanation
How we make knowledge clear or show why something happens. Knowing that the sky is blue is a fact; showing why (blue light scatters most) is an explanation.
Interpretation
How we assign meaning to information or events. Two historians read the same 1914 letters — one blames Germany for WWI, the other the alliance system.
Justification
The reasons or logic given to accept a claim as valid. “The Earth is round because ships disappear hull-first over the horizon and we have photos from space.”
Objectivity
The pursuit of knowledge free from personal or cultural bias. A double-blind drug trial: neither patients nor doctors know who got the real medicine.
Perspective
A specific point of view shaped by background or context. A factory closing is “cost saving” to the owner and a “lost livelihood” to the worker.
Power
How influence and authority control what knowledge is shared or trusted. Governments decide what goes into school history textbooks.
Responsibility
The ethical duties tied to how we create and use knowledge. The scientists who discovered nuclear fission had to consider whether it should be used for weapons.
Truth
Whether a statement or belief matches actual reality. “Sydney is the capital of Australia” is false (it’s Canberra), however many people believe it.
Values
The inner worth or importance we place on certain ideas or actions. One person values free speech most, another social harmony — so they disagree about banning offensive posts.

Types of knowledge

Procedural

Knowledge of how to do something.

Riding a bike or coding (slide examples).

Declarative

Knowledge that something is true — facts, theories and assertions.

Knowing that water is H₂O, or that WWII ended in 1945.

Experiential

Knowledge by acquaintance, gained directly through personal perception and involvement with something.

Knowing what a mango tastes like.

Subjective and objective perspectives

Knowledge claim
A statement with which we claim to know something. As individual knowers, our perspective on claims is subjective and so open to personal bias. “I know that my school has 600 students.”
Subjective
A personal view influenced by the knower’s opinions, feelings or emotions. “This song is the best song ever written.”
Objective
A detached view that focuses on facts in a way largely independent of the knower’s personal perspective, and that expects to be corroborated by a knowledge community. “This song was streamed 2 billion times in 2024” — anyone can check it.
Michael Polanyi
People like to believe knowledge is objective (agreed by many people with no emotional attachment to it). But all knowledge claims rely on personal judgement to an extent — we can’t rid ourselves of the era and culture we live in.
  • Attempts to free knowledge from all assumptions (tribal, cultural, linguistic) are called a “view from nowhere”.
  • But there is no view from nowhere: all knowledge is contextual and depends on knowers with assumptions and perspectives.
19th-century scientists “measured” skulls to “prove” racial differences — their era’s assumptions shaped what they believed was objective.

Fact, opinion or interpretation?

Fact

Can be checked against evidence.

Water boils at 100 °C at sea level. The unemployment rate increased by 2%.

Opinion

Expresses a judgement or preference.

Shakespeare was the greatest writer of all time. Van Gogh’s Sunflowers is beautiful.

Interpretation

Explains meaning or offers an explanation of events.

History is written by the victors. Climate change is caused by human activity (an interpretation strongly supported by evidence).

Objectivity in the DP subjects

Models and theories discussed in class: Psychology — the “Four Horsemen of the Apocalypse” (Gottman); Economics — the Invisible Hand (Adam Smith) vs. government intervention; Chemistry — atomic models; Maths — imaginary numbers; History — Great Man theory vs. social history; English — reader-response theory.

  • What makes this model/theory objective? What parts involve subjectivity or interpretation? What evidence supports it?
  • Assignment KQ: To what extent can a model/theory be claimed to be objective?
Lesson 5

Evaluating Information

Source: TOK1 Lesson 5 Evaluating information slides + your notes

The reliability of information and knowledge: we are presented with huge amounts of knowledge all the time — when, and how much, do we stop to check it?

Confirmation bias
We tend to be less sceptical about information on social media: it is our bubble, where our suspicions and beliefs are backed up. If you already think a diet works, you believe every success story and scroll past the studies against it — and the algorithm shows you even more success stories.
Propaganda
Advertising that promotes specific ideas and perspectives that are usually political. Wartime posters showing the enemy as monsters; state TV that only shows the leader being cheered.
Misinformation
Information that is distorted or only partially true. Your aunt shares “drinking hot water kills the virus” because she honestly believes it.
Disinformation
False information deliberately designed to mislead or confuse. A troll farm uses thousands of fake accounts to spread invented claims about an election candidate.
Fake news
Fabricated stories presented as genuine news items. A site made to look like the BBC publishes “Celebrity X arrested” — invented to get clicks.
The key difference between misinformation and disinformation is intent: misinformation is usually spread without meaning to deceive; disinformation is spread on purpose. Propaganda is also wider than advertising — it includes any biased communication pushing a cause.

Tools to check information

  • Snopes — checks disinformation and urban legends from social media.
  • The Big Project — finds media outlets around the world so you can compare different accounts of the same event.
  • Information vs. ideas/arguments (book p. 9–10): first decide whether an article is information or an idea/argument, then evaluate it with the matching set of considerations.
  • Is assessing the reliability of information harder or easier today than 50 years ago? Argue both sides.
  • What criteria did you use to decide that something is an example of propaganda / misinformation / disinformation / fake news?
Lesson 6

What Is Knowledge?

Source: TOK1 Lesson 6 What is knowledge slides + your notes

Plato: Justified True Belief (JTB)

Justified
Shown to be fair or reasonable. “It rained last night — the streets are wet and there are puddles everywhere.”
True
Logically consistent, honest, correct and accurate. “The Moon orbits the Earth” is true. “The Sun orbits the Earth” once seemed justified, but was never true — so never knowledge.
Belief
A feeling that what you think is true. You believe your friend is in Paris (belief), they are (true), and they sent you a photo at the Eiffel Tower today (justified) → you know it.
“Beyond reasonable doubt” only in your notes
Used in courts → how much certainty is enough? A jury can convict on DNA evidence + CCTV + a witness without being 100% certain.
  • Is it possible to know something but not believe it? Would it still count as knowledge?
Gettier case
A person can have a justified true belief and still fail to have knowledge, because their belief is true by luck — right for the wrong reasons. You look at a clock showing 3:00 and believe it’s 3:00. It is — but the clock stopped exactly 12 hours ago. Do you know the time?
God’s eye view
A hypothetical viewpoint from which objective truths can be perfectly perceived — also called an Archimedean point. The ideal of removing yourself from the object of study so you can see it in relation to everything else while staying independent of it: “seeing something completely from nowhere, with no bias, no feelings, no personal perspective.” Seeing a car accident from directly above with every detail, instead of from the pavement where a truck blocked half your view.
Can we ever see reality objectively? Is a God’s eye view (A) possible in real life, (B) never possible, or (C) only an ideal we can get closer to?
Paradigm
A world view underlying the theories and methodology of a particular scientific subject. (Thomas Kuhn.)
  • Paradigm → German Paradebeispiel (a model example) — a framework. only in your notes
“The discovery of universal gravitation became the paradigm of successful science.”
Paradigm shift
A major change in how people think about and understand a topic — so big that the accepted explanations, methods or “common sense” no longer work, and a new framework replaces the old one.

Paradigm shift examples (from the slides)

Natural sciences: Heliocentrism

Old: Earth at the centre of the universe. New (Copernicus, later supported): the Sun at the centre — changing how motion and observations were explained.

Psychology: Behaviourism

Old: focus on inner mental states and introspection. New: study observable behaviour and measurable stimulus–response patterns.

Politics: Nuclear weapons

Old: wars could be fought without risk of total annihilation. New: nuclear deterrence and mutually assured destruction changed how nations understand “war”, “peace” and risk.

Lesson 7

Types & Levels of Knowledge

Source: TOK1 Lesson 7 Types and levels of knowledge slides + your notes

Bloom’s Taxonomy (revised)

A way to describe how learning goes from basic to more complex thinking — like a ladder: at the bottom you learn facts, at the top you create new ideas. Slide example topic: photosynthesis.

  1. 1RememberMemorise facts and key terms (define a word, list dates, recall a method).
    Name the two raw materials plants use in photosynthesis.
  2. 2UnderstandExplain ideas in your own words (summarise, describe a chart, explain a cause).
    Explain in your own words how light helps a plant make glucose.
  3. 3ApplyUse what you learned in a new situation (solve a similar problem, use a formula).
    A plant is kept in a dark cupboard for a week — predict what happens to its rate of photosynthesis and why.
  4. 4AnalyseBreak things into parts and spot patterns (compare arguments, identify assumptions, separate evidence from opinion).
    One plant has plenty of light but little CO₂, the other the opposite — which factor limits each?
  5. 5EvaluateJudge using criteria: Is this good? Reliable? Strong evidence?
    Judge the claim “Adding more light will always make a plant photosynthesise faster.”
  6. 6CreateMake something new (write your own theory, design a plan, produce an original research question or model).
    Design an experiment to test whether light intensity affects photosynthesis.
Webb’s Depth of Knowledge (DoK) is similar but focuses on the actual difficulty/complexity of the task or prompt, not the type of thinking verb. You need to know the distinction for the exam! (Book p. 21, activity 2.3.) “List the planets” is low DoK; “Argue whether Pluto should be a planet, using evidence” is high DoK.

Tacit vs. explicit knowledge

Tacit

term from Michael Polanyi

Subjective, experiential knowledge that is difficult to express in words or communicate to others (e.g. craft skills, cognitive skills including your own perspective and values).

Explaining to a partner how to tie shoelaces without looking down (the slide exercise) — you can do it, but can’t easily put it into words.

Explicit

Knowledge that can be expressed in words or formulae (e.g. knowledge from books).

A recipe, the periodic table, IKEA instructions.
Centipede effect
Over-thinking an action so that it interferes with doing it. From the poem by Katherine Craster: a happy centipede is asked which leg moves after which, starts to think about it, and falls in a ditch, “not knowing how to run”.

Bertrand Russell (1872–1970): acquaintance vs. description

Knowledge by acquaintance
Direct experience that cannot be doubted — introspective experiences (feeling hungry), memory, and “universals” (e.g. roundness, beauty). Popular sense: knowledge acquired first-hand via our senses. “I know the taste of lemon because I’ve tasted one.”
Knowledge by description
About physical things such as people and places, which we can only know through descriptions. Popular sense: second-hand, mediated or inferential — from teachers or the news. “I know that lemons are sour because I’ve read that they taste that way.”
Russell: we understand descriptions because we know their parts by acquaintance — so knowledge by acquaintance underpins all knowledge by description. Languages mark the difference: French connaître (first-hand, to be acquainted with) vs. savoir (to know a fact, second-hand).

Levels of knowledge: breadth vs. depth

Knowing a little about 20 languages (breadth) vs. speaking one fluently (depth). A GP has breadth; a heart surgeon has depth.
  • The internet gives unlimited facts (and unlimited lies) instantly — does this mean the breadth of our knowledge expands easily?
  • How does the concept of falsehood link to the easy availability of knowledge?
  • With the web and AI, to what extent is depth or breadth more important?
Knowledge questions

Knowledge Questions (KQs)

Source: TOK1 KQs and map activity slides + Lesson 8 command terms slides + your notes

Knowledge question
A question about the nature of knowledge and what it means “to know”.
  • Focuses on TOK ideas rather than subject content, using TOK concepts like evidence, certainty, values and interpretation.
  • Originates in the real world and should be applicable to other real-world examples; even if philosophical, you must be able to bring it back to real situations and support it with real examples.
  • Open-ended and contestable — different perspectives, claims and counterclaims.
  • May link areas of knowledge, themes and the four elements: scope, perspectives, methods & tools, ethics.

From subject question to knowledge question

Three levels of thinking: (1) something you encounter in a subject → (2) a question about that subject → (3) a question about how knowledge is acquired, justified, evaluated or used.

ExampleSubject-specific questionKnowledge questionKey concepts
HistoryWhy do historians have different views of the 1917 October Revolution?To what extent can we still have reliable historical knowledge if, given access to the same facts, experts can disagree?Perspective, interpretation, objectivity
Natural sciencesWhy does the mass of some elements (magnesium) increase when heated?To what extent can we make accurate measurements that lead to reliable knowledge in the natural sciences?Evidence, certainty, justification
MapsHow do cartographers create maps?To what extent can knowledge be both accurate and useful?Perspective, truth, values
Moving to a KQ, questions become less about one subject or fact, more about the nature, production and limits of knowledge, open to different answers, and explorable with examples from several areas. We move from “what do we know?” to “how do we know?” and “how reliable is that knowledge?”

Map activity: can knowledge be both accurate and useful?

Accuracy
How closely a map represents what it claims to represent. A satellite photo of Helsinki is very accurate about what the city looks like.
Usefulness
How effectively a map serves its intended purpose. The Helsinki metro map distorts distances and shapes, but is far more useful for finding your train.
“The map is not the territory”
A representation never captures reality completely. A map isn’t necessarily inaccurate because it leaves things out — it may be deliberately selective to fulfil its purpose. But the representations we use may shape, not just reflect, our knowledge of reality: we often mistake our models, maps and theories for reality itself. The Mercator projection makes Greenland look as big as Africa (Africa is ~14× larger) — useful for sea navigation, misleading about size.
  • Accurate in what sense? Useful for whom, and for what purpose?
  • Who decides what is important enough to include? Does simplifying information necessarily make it less accurate?
  • Are accuracy and usefulness competing priorities, or can they complement each other?

Command terms in KQs

Command words can be explicit or implicit — always check what the KQ is asking you to do.

“Why do we pursue and share knowledge?” → explicit: explain why.
“In what ways should our values and ethical responsibilities influence how we produce and share knowledge?” → implicit: explore the various ways.
Lesson 8

The Knowledge Framework & Scope

Source: TOK1 Lesson 8 KQs and KF scope slides + your notes

The knowledge framework (KF) is a tool for analysing and comparing areas of knowledge — like a map that helps you find your way and build links between them. Every KQ can be answered using its four strands.

Scope

  • What is this AOK or theme about? What makes it intriguing?
  • Its strengths and limitations?
  • What is studied, what problems can it solve? Where are its frontiers?
  • Current open, unanswered or contentious questions?
History studies the human past. Limitation: most ordinary people in the past left no records.

Perspectives

  • Role of the individual knower, and of the individual within the community of knowers?
  • How important are different perspectives? What responsibilities do contributors have?
  • Key concepts, key terms and metaphors?
A British and an Indian historian may describe British rule in India very differently.

Methods & tools

  • Conventions and rituals? Methods and procedures?
  • Skills or initiations needed? Underlying assumptions?
  • Role of models? Role of technology?
Historians compare primary sources, check who wrote them and why, and cite evidence in footnotes.

Ethics

  • What ethical considerations or responsibilities arise in the pursuit of knowledge within this AOK?
Should a historian publish a diary revealing secrets of people whose grandchildren are still alive?

Scope in detail

Scope
The subject content of an area of knowledge: what it tries to know, why that matters, and where its boundaries are. It looks at how the area fits into the whole of human knowledge, the problems it addresses, and how.
  • What is the AOK about, what defines its shared knowledge, and why do we seek knowledge in it?
  • The extent of the AOK, its limits, and how it sits within the total of knowledge.
Mathematics is about ideas such as shape, number and pattern.
What shapes your view of scope
Personal experience, the IB curriculum, culture, type of education. If your school taught only European history, you may assume “history” means mainly European history.
Applications
Different AOKs (and fields within them) have different applications. Making sure a bridge won’t collapse (physics, maths); understanding human consciousness (psychology); solving the climate crisis (natural and human sciences).
Lesson 10

Knowledge Framework: Perspectives

Source: TOK1 Lesson 10 KF perspectives slides + your notes

Perspective
= viewpoint. Each knower has a unique perspective on the world, and hence on knowledge. A farmer and a tourist on the same rainy day: “good for the crops” vs. “ruined holiday”.
Influences on perspective
As well as individual context and history: faith, paradigms and laws. Perspective is also historical — the shared knowledge accepted in any community changes over time. Your grandparents at 17 probably had very different views on gender roles or the environment — largely because of the historical context.

Hofstede’s cultural dimensions

A framework for comparing broad patterns in cultural values across countries. Hofstede built it from surveys of IBM employees in different countries (1967–1973), using statistics to find recurring patterns (four original dimensions; long-term orientation and indulgence were added later). The dimensions come from patterns in survey data — they are not fixed rules about how every person in a country thinks or behaves.

DimensionMeaningHigh ↑ / Low ↓Example
Power distanceAttitudes toward hierarchy and inequality.↑ the less powerful expect and accept unequal powerHigh: Malaysian students rarely question teachers. Low: Danish students use teachers’ first names and debate them.

↓ “we”, strong ties		High: USA — choose a career for personal fulfilment. Low: China — family expectations matter more.
Masculinity – femininity (“motivation towards achievement & success”)Competition and achievement vs. care and cooperation.↑ competitive

Ecological fallacy
Wrongly assuming that what is true of a group on average is true of every individual in it. This is the key limitation of Hofstede: country scores describe averages, not people. Japan scores high on collectivism, so assuming your Japanese classmate must dislike standing out is an ecological fallacy — they may be very individualistic.

Case study: the international project meeting

Anna (Germany) arrives with an agenda and openly criticises weak ideas; Marcus (USA) volunteers to lead and says “let’s be flexible”; Luana (Brazil) wants to get to know each other first and avoids open conflict; Kenji (Japan) says “That may be difficult” — meaning serious disagreement — and later waits for clearer instructions from the leader.

A cultural reading: Kenji’s indirectness fits collectivism and high power distance; Anna’s plan fits high uncertainty avoidance. A non-cultural reading: Kenji may simply be shy, anxious about his English, or new to group work. Both are possible — avoid treating one culture as if everyone shares one perspective.
  • When does a cultural framework improve our understanding of behaviour, and when does it turn a useful generalisation into a stereotype?
  • To what extent can we understand another person’s perspective without imposing our own cultural assumptions?
  • How does our cultural perspective influence the way we interpret an object?
  • Open-minded vs. internationally-minded: to what extent are they the same?
Lesson 11

Knowledge Framework: Methods & Tools

Source: Lesson 11 KF tools and methods slides + your notes

In TOK, “methods and tools” are the ways people produce, test, justify, communicate and challenge knowledge. Key question: What methods and tools are used to create knowledge in this area, and how reliable or limited are they?

Method
A systematic way of investigating or developing knowledge. Being systematic does not make it automatically reliable — ask: what assumptions does it make? What can it show and not show? Who decides if it worked? How might bias affect results? Experiments, analysing historical documents, interviews, surveys, mathematical proof, interpreting a poem or religious text, observing behaviour, statistical analysis, testing a hypothesis, corroborating evidence from several sources.
Tools (ways of knowing)
The mental, emotional, linguistic or perceptual resources used in knowing. A historian uses memory, language and reason as tools, and source evaluation and corroboration as methods.

Tools: what they contribute and their limits

What is a “good reason” to accept a claim?

Slide exercise — claim: “This photograph shows a dangerous situation.” Rank these reasons:

Intuition

“I immediately felt that something was wrong.”

Evidence

“There is visible smoke, damaged property and people running.”

Authority

“A trained emergency responder said it was dangerous.”

Consensus

“Everyone in the group agreed that it looked dangerous.”

  • Is one type of justification always stronger than the others? Could an apparently convincing reason still be unreliable?
  • Use qualified conclusions: “We accept the claim provisionally because…”, “…convincing in some contexts, but not universally.”

Inductive & deductive reasoning

Reasoning is the tool that moves us from evidence or premises to a conclusion.

DeductiveInductive
DirectionGeneral rule → specific caseSpecific observations → general conclusion
ConclusionIf the premises are true and the reasoning valid, it must be trueProbable, not certain; open to revision
ExampleAll metals expand when heated. Copper is a metal. → Copper expands when heated.
All mammals are warm-blooded. Whales are mammals. → Whales are warm-blooded.		The last five swans I saw were white → all swans are probably white (black swans exist in Australia!).
The sun has risen every morning so far → it will probably rise tomorrow.
Is one better? Neither is automatically better. Deduction is only certain if its premises are true; induction is common in science and everyday life because it lets us generalise from evidence. It depends on the type of claim and the quality of the premises or evidence.

Reasoning links to other tools

  • Evidence — induction depends on observations or patterns in evidence.
  • Assumptions — deduction depends on the truth of its starting premises. “All teenagers are lazy. Sam is a teenager. So Sam is lazy.” Valid logic, false premise.
  • Authority — experts may use induction or deduction to support claims.
  • Consensus — a conclusion may be accepted when different people independently reach it by similar reasoning.
  • Intuition — may be based on rapid, unconscious inductive reasoning, though hard to explain or justify (“System 1” thinking, Daniel Kahneman). You walk into a room and instantly “feel” that your friends were just arguing.
  • “A good reason is not necessarily a certain reason.” Discuss.
  • “The reliability of a tool for acquiring knowledge depends on the context in which it is used.” To what extent do you agree?
Key topic

Relativism & Absolutism

Source: your handwritten notes; the Ethics slides refer to relativism and cultural relativism

Relativity
Recognising that knowledge claims depend on contextual factors, frames or references. “The train is moving at 100 km/h” — relative to the ground. To a passenger, their coffee cup isn’t moving at all.
Relativism
The belief that what is true or right for one person or group is not necessarily true or right for another → all truths are of equal value. “Eating meat is wrong for a Hindu vegetarian but fine for a Mongolian herder — neither is more correct.”
Absolutism
Belief in absolute truth and absolute cultural, religious, political and moral standards against which all other views can be judged. “Torture is always wrong, in every culture and situation, no exceptions.”
Cultural relativism
Shows how culture influences the way we produce, interpret and evaluate knowledge; beliefs, values and practices should be understood within their cultural context. Encourages intellectual humility: think before judging another society (criticism is still allowed). Eating with your hands may look impolite to a European, but in India or Ethiopia it’s normal and has its own etiquette.
Ethnocentrism
Thinking that your own culture is superior. Colonial-era Europeans calling other societies “primitive”.
Tension: respect for cultural differences vs. universal judgement (e.g. human rights apply to everyone regardless of culture).
Theories of truth

Theories of Truth only in your notes

Source: your handwritten notes. Running example: “It is raining outside.”

Correspondence

Aristotle

True if it corresponds to a fact — a relation with the real world.

True if you look outside and water really is falling.

Coherence

True if it fits consistently into our overall system of beliefs — a relation within a set of beliefs.

It fits everything else: the forecast said rain, people have umbrellas, you hear drops on the roof.

Pragmatic

William James

True if it is useful and works in practice.

Acting on it works: you take an umbrella and stay dry.

Consensus

True if the majority of people believe it.

Everyone in the room agrees. Problem: most people once agreed the Earth was flat.

Pluralist

Truth has multiple meanings depending on the context or area of knowledge.

Science: matching observation. Maths: fitting the axioms.

Redundancy

Frank Ramsey

“It is true that p” means exactly the same as “p” — the word “true” adds nothing.

“It is true that it is raining” = “It is raining.”
Lessons 12 + 13

Ethics

Source: TOK1 Lesson 12 and 13 Ethics slides + your notes

Ethics in TOK
Concerned with whether or not we can have moral knowledge, and what the best course of action is under different ethical systems. Can we know stealing is wrong the way we know 2 + 2 = 4? Would every system agree that stealing bread for a starving child is wrong?
Relativism in ethics
Right and wrong differ between countries and cultures. If we can’t agree, is ethics just personal opinion? Eating beef is ordinary in Finland but deeply wrong for many Hindus in India.
Agency
Conscious, social beings making intentional, often difficult decisions about how to act in the real world. You see a classmate being bullied and choose whether to step in, tell a teacher or stay silent.
Rules ≠ morals
Rules and conventions guide us towards “right” behaviour, but rules are not morals — and ethics is not the same as manners and convention. Racial segregation in the US was once the law; following it was legal but not moral. Eating with your elbows on the table breaks a convention, not an ethical rule.
Conscience
Our personal awareness of what is right and wrong. Our social conscience is our awareness of our ethical obligations and responsibilities towards others. The guilty feeling after lying to a friend, even if nobody found out.
To what extent does your idea of right and wrong come from your conscience, and to what extent is your conscience just what you’ve been taught?
Altruism only in your notes
Helping others over oneself, with no benefit to oneself, even at a cost. Donating a kidney to a stranger.

Methods for gaining ethical knowledge

Reason

There can be rational reasons for our feelings of right and wrong; general moral principles (e.g. the Golden Rule); a biological basis for cooperation, pro-social behaviour, altruism and selfishness. Not always clear-cut.

Euthanasia: the Hippocratic oath vs. the patient’s own wish.

Emotion / intuition

Repulsion towards horrific acts; “gut feeling”. Can these ever be a reliable measure of right and wrong?

“Sniffy the Rat” (early 1990s): artist Rick Gibson planned to crush a pet-shop rat (which might otherwise have been snake food) to start a debate on killing animals. Activists rescued it — emotion vs. reason.

Faith

God-given codes or divine commands. The Golden Rule (since Confucius): treat others as you would like to be treated. But faith and morality are distinct — being religious isn’t the same as being moral; arguably how we live matters more than what we believe.

Horrific acts committed “in the name of religion”, including modern terrorism.

Ethical frames

In short: utilitarianism focuses on consequences, deontology on duties, virtue ethics on character.

Utilitarianism

consequences

An action is right if it produces the greatest overall happiness or benefit for the greatest number of people.

A doctor gives a scarce treatment to the patient most likely to recover. Pop culture: Thanos (Infinity War) wipes out half of life to “prevent suffering” — utilitarian reasoning, but a deeply questionable calculation.

Deontology

duties

An action is right or wrong based on duties, rules or principles — not only its consequences.

You shouldn’t lie even if lying gives a better outcome, because honesty is a duty. Pop culture: Harry Potter refuses to kill even when it would be strategically useful.

Virtue ethics

character

An action is right if it reflects good character traits or virtues such as honesty, courage, compassion and fairness.

You return a lost wallet because an honest, responsible person would. Pop culture: Atticus Finch (To Kill a Mockingbird) defends Tom Robinson with courage and integrity despite social pressure.

Categorical imperative — Immanuel Kant

An objective, rationally necessary and unconditional principle that all rational agents must follow, despite any desires to the contrary: “Act only on a maxim you could will to become a universal law.”

Your rule: “I may make promises I don’t intend to keep.” If everyone did this, nobody would believe promises, so promising would stop existing — the rule defeats itself. So breaking promises is wrong.

Scenarios for applying the frames

1. The runaway trolley

Pull a lever to divert a trolley from five people onto one person?

2. The stolen medicine

A parent can’t afford medicine that would save their child; should they steal it?

3. The group project

A member did almost no work because of serious problems at home — give equal credit, report them, or leave their name off?

Method: list the possible actions → apply each frame → compare whether they agree → judge which is most convincing. A good outcome doesn’t automatically make an action morally right.

Knowledge questions on ethics

  • Are there responsibilities that necessarily come with knowing something or knowing how to do something?
  • Is it justified for ethical considerations to limit the production of knowledge?
  • Do knowers have a moral duty to examine their own filters?
  • Under what circumstances, if any, do we have a moral duty to share what we know?
  • In what ways do ethical judgements differ from other kinds of judgements?
  • Can moral disagreements be resolved with reference to empirical evidence?
  • Is there such a thing as a moral fact?
  • If moral claims conflict, does it follow that all views are equally acceptable?
  • What personal traits do we need to be ethical knowers?
